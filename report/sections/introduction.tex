\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Consider six positions around a table. Positions 0 and 5 are adjacent, just
as positions 0 and 1 are. If an edge of a graph joins two vertices, their
separation in such a layout is the length of the shorter arc between their
positions. The cyclic bandwidth minimization problem (CBMP) asks how small
the largest of these edge separations can be when each vertex receives a
distinct position.

A layout gives an upper bound that can be checked by inspecting its edges.
A claim of optimality also requires evidence that every layout with a smaller
bandwidth is impossible. This distinction matters when a solver reaches its
time limit. A run can yield a useful layout even while the optimum remains
unknown.

We study three questions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item How do direct pairwise and order encodings compare in variable count,
      clause count, construction time and time to prove optimality?
\item On identical graphs and under a common wall-clock budget, which
      methods find the smallest validated bandwidth and which close the
      gap to the lower bound?
\item How do graph size, edge density and maximum degree affect model
      construction, memory use and the bounds available at timeout?
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:pairwise} gives the direct encoding, its size at a fixed
threshold and the search procedure used in the pilot. The experimental
comparison uses small graphs with exhaustive reference answers to check
correctness, and graphs derived from Harwell--Boeing matrices to study
performance.
